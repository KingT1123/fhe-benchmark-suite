\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section presents the results obtained for SEAL. Latency figures are drawn from the Standard scenario; storage, noise budget, and CKKS error are properties of the parameters themselves and were measured once across the full grid (Section~\ref{sec:methodology}).

Table~\ref{tab:latency} gives the range of each operation's cost across every tested parameter combination. 38 of 144 Standard-scenario latency measurements were flagged \texttt{HIGH\_VARIANCE} -- expected for fast operations, where ordinary background noise is large relative to the operation itself.

Every operation is fast in absolute terms; the differences are relative. At $N{=}8192$ and category~1, an addition takes about 0.06\,ms in all three schemes, while key generation takes about 66\,ms, three orders of magnitude more. \subsection{Energy and memory}
\label{sec:energy-memory}

Table~\ref{tab:energy-memory} gives the range of energy per operation and peak memory across the Standard-scenario grid, measured with the dedicated protocol of Section~\ref{sec:energy-protocol}. \emph{Gross} energy is the processor package's total energy during the timed operations, divided by the number of operations; it needs no assumption. \emph{Net} energy subtracts the machine's idle power over the same time, estimating what the operation itself adds; it depends on the idle baseline (13.5\,W, see Section~\ref{sec:energy-protocol}).

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering\small
\input{generated/energy_memory_table.tex}
\caption{Energy per operation and peak memory, Standard scenario, range over every measured parameter combination ($N \le 8192$ plus $N{=}16384$/category~1). Energy: mean of 30 repetitions per cell. Memory: mean of three separate runs per cell, each performing one operation at a time.}
\label{tab:energy-memory}
\end{table}

Energy follows latency closely: the operation adds about 7.5\,W above idle (median over cells; 4.6--15.4\,W), so net energy is roughly latency times a near-constant power, and the ordering of operations is the same as in Table~\ref{tab:latency}. A key generation at $N{=}16384$ costs about 2\,J net (5.8\,J gross), while an addition at $N{=}2048$ costs about $5{\times}10^{-5}$\,J.

Peak memory is the whole process's peak resident memory while performing one operation at a time: SEAL context, keys, the operands, and the result. It is small -- at most 22.4\,MiB at $N \le 8192$ and 121\,MiB at $N{=}16384$/category~1 -- and is dominated by the keys and ciphertexts, so multiply and relinearize (which need the relinearization keys and a three-component ciphertext) use the most.

Three observations follow from the data:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{BGV spends more budget per multiplication than BFV} at the same parameters: about 37--38 bits per round against 30--32 for BFV. As a result BFV supports one more multiplication than the chain length suggests at $N{=}8192$ (three rounds at category~1, two at category~3), while BGV supports exactly the chain's depth.
  \item \textbf{BGV at $N{=}4096$/category~1 sits at the edge of depth 1.} After its single multiplication the reported budget is 0 bits in the main run (SEAL rounds down, so this means less than one bit), yet every decryption was correct. Because SEAL draws fresh random keys in every run, this value depends on the key: 16 further independent runs (400 decryptions after the multiplication) left between 2 and 6 bits, and every decryption was correct. BFV at the same cell stays at 7--8 bits regardless of the key.
  \item \textbf{Three cells use a smaller plaintext modulus} ($N{=}2048$ categories 1 and 5, and $N{=}4096$ category~5; see Table~\ref{tab:configmeta-bfvbgv}), so their budgets are not directly comparable to the other cells' on that basis alone.
\end{itemize}

Figure~\ref{fig:ckkserror} gives the maximum absolute error after one addition for every CKKS cell (Table~\ref{tab:configmeta-ckks} gives the full values, including after the multiply chain).

\begin{figure}[h!]

\caption{CKKS maximum absolute error after one addition, every cell (log scale; inputs in $[-1,1]$).}

Error is lowest at $N{=}8192$ ($\approx 10^{-8}$ for all three categories, at most $1.1{\times}10^{-6}$ through the full multiply chain) and grows as the coefficient-modulus budget shrinks. At $N{=}2048$, category~1 is usable ($1.5{\times}10^{-4}$) after its scale was tuned; the problematic cells are category~3, whose 17-bit budget limits the error to about 8\% (a structural limit, Section~\ref{sec:security-validation}), and category~5, whose single 26-bit prime gives $2.1{\times}10^{-3}$ even at the largest scale SEAL accepts. Both of these should be read with that caveat rather than compared directly with the other cells.

\subsection{Composite workloads}
\label{sec:composite}

The previous results time each operation in isolation. Real applications chain operations, so the Composite scenario measures three chained workloads at $N{=}8192$, category~1 (the scope of this scenario), for all three schemes, with the same 100-repetition protocol: a \textbf{dot product}, a \textbf{polynomial evaluation}, and the \textbf{rotation} it relies on. Matrix--vector multiplication was removed from the scope of this stage with the supervisor's agreement (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{Building blocks.} Table~\ref{tab:composite-ops} gives the cost of one slot rotation, of generating the rotation (Galois) key it needs, and of evaluating the polynomial $2x^2 + 3x + 5$ element-wise (Horner's method, $(2x+3)x+5$: one plaintext multiplication, one ciphertext--ciphertext multiplication with relinearization, and two plaintext additions). BFV's polynomial evaluation is about $3$--$4\times$ slower than BGV's and CKKS's because of its ciphertext multiplication alone: 8.65\,ms for BFV against about 0.45\,ms for BGV and CKKS at this size (Standard scenario).

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering\small
\input{generated/composite_ops_table.tex}
\caption{Composite building blocks, $N{=}8192$/category~1 (mean of 100 repetitions). The Galois key covers only the single rotation step actually used.}
\label{tab:composite-ops}
\end{table}

\paragraph{Rotation keys.} SEAL's default \texttt{create\_galois\_keys()} generates a key for every power-of-two rotation step -- 24 keys, 37.8\,MB at this size -- even when a computation uses only one. The harness instead requests only the steps it uses: 1.64\,MB for a single step, 23$\times$ smaller (both sizes measured directly, uncompressed). This was an error in an earlier version of the harness, caught by the correctness pass, and is why key size must be reported together with the set of steps it covers.

\paragraph{Dot product and the prediction formula.} The dot product of two encrypted vectors of length $\ell$ is one multiplication, one relinearization, then $k = \lceil \log_2 \ell \rceil$ rounds of rotate-and-add that sum the products into one slot. If single-operation timings are enough to predict chained cost, then
\[
T_{\text{pred}} = T_{\text{mult}} + T_{\text{relin}} + k\,(T_{\text{rot}} + T_{\text{add}}),
\]
using the Standard-scenario multiply, relinearize and add latencies and the Composite rotate latency. Since the four measurements are independent, the uncertainty is propagated as
\[
\sigma^2_{\text{pred}} = \sigma^2_{\text{mult}} + \sigma^2_{\text{relin}} + k^2\,(\sigma^2_{\text{rot}} + \sigma^2_{\text{add}}).
\]
Prediction and measurement were compared with a two one-sided tests (TOST) equivalence test (Welch's $t$, $\alpha = 0.05$) with a margin of $\pm 10\%$ of the predicted value; the predicted side uses the multiply measurement's sample size ($n{=}100$) as a representative $n$, a documented approximation.

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering\small\setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
\input{generated/composite_prediction_table.tex}
\caption{Predicted vs.\ measured dot-product latency, $N{=}8192$/category~1. ``Diff.'' is measured minus predicted; the 90\% CI is the interval the TOST procedure uses. CKKS stops at length 4096, its slot count.}
\label{tab:composite-pred}
\end{table}

All twelve comparisons (Table~\ref{tab:composite-pred}) are equivalent within $\pm 10\%$, at every vector length and for all three schemes, so per-operation costs compose additively into the chain's cost. The formula slightly overestimates in 11 of the 12 cells (by 0.4--4.9\%; in most cells the confidence interval excludes zero); a plausible reason is that in a chain the operations run back-to-back on data already in cache, which isolated timings do not benefit from.

\paragraph{Noise in the chain, and isolated controls.} Every BFV and BGV dot product decrypted correctly (840 of 840 repetitions checked against the exact result). To attribute the chain's noise-budget use to its parts, two control traces repeat only the $k$ rotations or only the $k$ additions of the chain, from a fresh ciphertext (10 trials each, all decrypting correctly). Table~\ref{tab:composite-noise} shows that rotations and additions are cheap -- at most about 5 and 3.4 bits over 13 steps -- so the multiplication at the start of the chain (about 32 bits for BFV and 37 for BGV at this size, Table~\ref{tab:noisebudget}) dominates the chain's noise cost.

\subsection{Behavior under different conditions}

\subsubsection*{Resource constraints}

The Constrained scenario caps the container at 4 cores and 4\,GB of memory. On the common $N \le 8192$ grid, both scenarios produced the same pattern -- 126 configurations measured, 36 skipped (depth 0), none failed. Table~\ref{tab:constrained} compares them cell by cell, as the ratio Constrained/Standard.

\begin{table}[h!]
\centering\small
\input{generated/std_vs_constrained_table.tex}
\caption{Constrained vs.\ Standard on the 126 common cells ($N \le 8192$). Latency comes from the main timing runs; energy and memory from the dedicated energy/memory runs, with the same protocol in both scenarios.}
\label{tab:constrained}
\end{table}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*, itemsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Memory is unaffected.} Peak memory differs by at most 6\% in any cell (2\% lower under Constrained in median), and at most 22.4\,MiB -- about 0.5\% of the 4\,GB cap, which therefore never binds at this workload scale.
  \item \textbf{Latency is close.} Constrained was 5\% faster in median in the main timing runs, but about 5\% slower in the energy runs; differences of this size change direction between runs, so they reflect run-to-run variation on this machine rather than an effect of the cap. No formal equivalence test was applied, so this is a descriptive observation.
  \item \textbf{Energy was higher under Constrained} by 14\% in median (gross; 27\% net), from about 5\% longer operations and 8\% higher package power. The two runs were made one after the other on the same day (Standard 03:16--05:55, Constrained 06:03--08:35, mean processor temperature 88.5 vs.\ 89.7\,$^\circ$C), so this difference cannot be attributed to the resource cap alone; it should be read as indicative.
  \item \textbf{Timing variance is the same:} \texttt{HIGH\_VARIANCE} counts are nearly identical (30 vs.\ 32 of 126), so pinning to fixed cores did not measurably reduce timing variance in this campaign.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Batching}
\input{generated/batching_numbers.tex}

The Edge/Batch scenario processes 1, 10 or 100 items per request. Key generation is done once per request, so its cost per item falls in proportion to the batch size (Figure~\ref{fig:batching}; this curve is the measured key-generation time divided by the batch size, not a separate measurement). For the recurring operations, the per-item latency at batch size 100 relative to batch size 1 has a median ratio of \batchRatioMedian{} (range \batchRatioMin--\batchRatioMax{} over \batchN{} cells). A TOST equivalence test ($\pm$10\%) finds the two equivalent in \batchEquiv{} of \batchN{} cells; of the remaining \batchNotEstablished, \batchRealDiff{} show a difference beyond the margin, in both directions (faster in some cells, slower in others) and mostly for the fastest operations, whose batch-size-100 runs have only 10 repetitions. There is therefore no consistent per-item speed-up from batching: each item is already processed in its most SIMD-efficient form from the first ciphertext.

\begin{figure}[h!]
\centering
\input{generated/batching_figure.tex}
\caption{Key-generation cost per item vs.\ batch size, $N{=}8192$/category~1 (log--log): measured key-generation time divided by batch size.}
\label{fig:batching}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Practical implication:} generate keys once and reuse them across every future operation; batching requests together does not reduce the cost of the underlying cryptographic operations.

\subsubsection*{Packing}

The Packing scenario asks whether a ciphertext that carries fewer real values is cheaper. At $N{=}8192$/category~1, encrypt, add and multiply were timed with every slot holding a real value and with a single real value (the rest zero), as well as at 10\% and 50\% fill. Table~\ref{tab:packing} compares the two extremes. Cost does not depend on how many slots are used: eight of nine comparisons are equivalent within $\pm$10\%, and the ninth (CKKS add, $+10.7\%$) is inconclusive rather than different, because of its high timing variance. The serialized ciphertext is also 393{,}329 bytes at every fill level. Packing as many values as possible into each ciphertext is therefore free, and leaving slots empty saves nothing.
